Background:
Singapore imports over 90% of its food, making household grocery bills highly vulnerable to global supply chain disruptions, energy costs, and geopolitical shocks. At the same time, Singapore’s
unique dining culture, anchored by ubiquitous hawker centres and coffee shops, means eating out is an everyday necessity rather than a discretionary luxury for most residents. As a result, food
represents one of the largest ongoing expenses in household budgets.
Between 2015 and 2025, Singapore transitioned from a period of mild, stable inflation (2015–2019) into a post-2020 environment characterised by compounding price pressures. Global grain and
fertiliser shortages, climate-related export curbs, and freight spikes combined with domestic cost increases; including rising commercial rents, higher utility tariffs, wage adjustments, and GST
changes.
Using official monthly Consumer Price Index (CPI) data from the Singapore Department of Statistics (SingStat Table M213751), this report tracks food price changes over this 10-year span. It
evaluates whether food price inflation outpaced general headline inflation and analyses how the cost of dining out diverged from home grocery preparation.

Methods:
Data Source and Preparation Data was retrieved from the Singapore Department of Statistics (SingStat Table M213751: Consumer Price Index, 2024 Base Year).
The raw table was reshaped from wide to long format (category, year, month, cpi_index), non-numeric historical entries were removed, and category names were cleaned of formatting spaces. The
dataset was verified against SingStat’s published figures, confirming that the 2024 annual average for "All Items" equals exactly 100.0. Analysis and Calculations To measure long-term price
trends, monthly indices were averaged for each year from 2015 to 2025. Cumulative 10-year percentage changes were calculated by comparing 2025 annual averages against 2015 baseline
averages.
Food categories were divided into two main groups: Eating Out: Food & Beverage Serving Services (including hawker centres, fast food, and restaurants). Food at Home: Groceries (including
meat, vegetables, seafood, fruits, and staples). Newly added categories that lacked continuous historical data back to 2015 were excluded from the 10-year comparison.

Results:
Food Outpaced General Inflation Between 2015 and 2025, overall food prices in Singapore rose by 27.7%, significantly outpacing the headline inflation rate (All Items), which rose by 18.6% over
the same period. Dining Out Rose Faster Than Groceries Eating out rose faster than buying groceries for home cooking: Eating out (Food & Beverage Serving Services) rose by 28.7%. Food at
home (Food Excl Food & Beverage Serving Services) rose by 25.7%. Both categories increased at similar rates from 2015 to 2020 (around 8% to 9%), but diverged after 2021 as dining-out prices
accelerated due to rising wage, rental, and utility costs. Breakdown by Venue and Food Type Dining Venues: Price increases were highest in Fast Food Restaurants (+30.5%), followed by Hawker
Centres and Coffee Shops (+28.9%) and full-service Restaurants (+28.3%). Groceries: Perishables led grocery inflation, with Vegetables (+30.5%), Fruits & Nuts (+27.8%), and Meat (+27.7%)
seeing the highest cumulative increases. In contrast, Fish & Seafood experienced a lower increase of 18.8%.

Limitations:
Substitution Bias (Fixed Basket Assumption): The CPI tracks price changes for a fixed basket of goods based on official household expenditure surveys. It does not reflect changes in real-world
consumer behaviour, such as households substituting expensive meats (e.g., mutton) with cheaper alternatives (e.g., chicken), or trading down from full-service restaurants to hawker centres
during inflationary periods.
2024 Basket Reclassification and Category Exclusions: When SingStat rebased the index to 2024, it introduced 45 new and reclassified granular categories (such as separating Restaurants,
Cafes, and Pubs, and splitting Hawker Centres from Food Courts). Because these new subcategories lack continuous data prior to 2024, they were excluded from the 10-year cumulative trend
analysis, restricting long-term tracking to continuous, higher-level series.
Portion Sizes and "Shrinkflation": While the Department of Statistics applies quality adjustments to track standard quantities, subtle changes in portion sizes, ingredient quality, or packaging
reductions in food service and packaged groceries may not be fully captured in pure price index movements. Regional and Venue Price Dispersion: The CPI aggregates island-wide monthly
sampling. As a result, it reflects national averages and may obscure localised price differences between central business district dining versus heartland neighbourhood hawker centres.
